Coming into this lab, my main assumption was that giving an AI model examples, asking it to think step-by-step, or routing difficult tickets to a larger model would naturally improve our results. We spent Lab 2 putting that intuition to the test on real customer support emails from Aurora Insurance. The goal was to automatically pull out key details like category, urgency, sentiment, and customer info, while carefully comparing the trade-offs between extraction accuracy, API expenses, and latency.

To test this, I built several configurations in code. We started with a clean baseline that relied strictly on prompt definitions and code-based regex rules. From there, I set up a few-shot setup using six specific edge cases that are difficult to explain in text alone, such as distinguishing an angry refund request from a normal billing inquiry or spotting Hindi phrases in English messages. I also designed a reasoned prompt that deliberately rearranged the schema to make the AI generate its reasoning before selecting answers, ensuring its thoughts actually influenced its decisions. Finally, I put together a cascade pipeline that used the budget-friendly small model first and escalated to the larger model whenever two samples taken at different temperatures disagreed with each other.

Running all of these against our sixty test tickets delivered a massive reality check. Our simplest setup—the small zero-shot model—turned out to be the clear champion. It achieved a 75% field accuracy and a 23.3% record accuracy while costing only thirty-five cents per thousand tickets and answering in about one second. In comparison, adding six detailed examples barely nudged record accuracy to 26.7% while hiking the price by over forty percent. Making the model write out its reasoning was even more wasteful; it caused output tokens to skyrocket from roughly 58 to 126 per ticket, costing more than double without adding a single percentage point to record accuracy. Because the initial prompt was already well-defined, these extra prompting tricks just burned tokens on things the model already understood.

The statistical analysis showed why taking percentage gains at face value is dangerous. A bump from 23.3% to 26.7% sounds like progress, but running a paired McNemar test yielded a p-value of 0.7266. In plain terms, there is over a seventy percent chance that this tiny gap was pure luck on a small sample of sixty tickets. The confidence intervals for every single model overlapped heavily, proving that none of the fancy prompts produced a genuine, repeatable improvement.

The cascade experiment revealed an even deeper lesson about how language models fail. We expected the cascade to catch hard tickets, but it ended up escalating 43.3% of all cases, doubling our processing costs and causing massive latency spikes with no accuracy payoff. When I looked into why, the numbers showed it was escalating right answers and wrong answers at practically identical rates (around 43%). The model's primary struggle was urgency, where it scored just 35% accuracy because it habitually guessed an urgency of two and avoided extremes like one or five. Since the model made this same biased mistake whether the temperature was set to zero or 0.7, the self-consistency test never detected any disagreement. The system assumed the model was confident, when in reality it was just confidently biased.

This experiment completely changed how I think about building AI products. It proved that adding complexity, examples, or multi-model cascades can easily hurt your budget and speed without helping your accuracy at all. Moving forward, the right approach is to always deploy the simplest viable baseline, check whether errors stem from uncertainty or baked-in bias before designing fixes, and rely on solid statistical tests rather than gut feelings to judge what works.
